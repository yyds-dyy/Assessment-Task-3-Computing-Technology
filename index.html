About CADRE

Purpose and Personal Interest

Purpose

What is the purpose of CADRE rovers?

NASA’s CADRE (Cooperative Autonomous Distributed Robotic Exploration) system aims to demonstrate that three rovers can explore the Moon as an autonomous team. Its purpose is to test whether robots can coordinate their decisions and collect scientific measurements with limited human supervision, establishing technology for future space exploration.

  • Demonstrate independent teamwork. Operators on Earth will provide an overall objective, while the rovers will divide tasks, share information and select safe routes. This transfers responsibility for routine coordination to the robots, testing whether a team can pursue a shared goal without humans directing every movement.

  • Demonstrate scientific capabilities that require multiple robots. The rovers will map the lunar surface and use ground-penetrating radar to investigate beneath it. Taking coordinated measurements from different locations simultaneously enables them to gather data that a single rover could not collect alone. Cooperation therefore expands the kinds of scientific investigations robots can perform.

  • Advance A-PUFFER’s technology into lunar exploration. CADRE builds on A-PUFFER (Autonomous Pop-Up Flat Folding Explorer Robot) , which developed an early version of its cooperative autonomy software. CADRE’s purpose includes assessing whether Earth-based testing accurately predicts performance on the Moon—providing evidence that the technology can operate under actual mission conditions.

  • One planned autonomy experiment requires the rovers to independently select routes through a designated area of approximately 400m^2, while their stereo-camera observations contribute to a three dimensional topographic map. In another experiment, their multistatic ground-penetrating radars are intended to investigate structures as deep as approximately beneath the lunar surface.These experiments test not only whether the robots can move autonomously, but whether cooperation between several autonomous machines can produce useful information that would be difficult for a single rover to obtain.

Personal Interest

Why did I choose CADRE?

I selected CADRE because I am particularly interested in how several robots can operate as one coordinated system rather than as independently controlled machines.

A conventional rover receives instructions from human operators and makes some local navigation decisions. CADRE advances this idea by requiring three rovers to communicate, share data, allocate tasks and coordinate their movements while managing limited energy and operating time. This makes the system an interesting intersection of artificial intelligence, robotics, communications and mechanical engineering.

I am also interested in the use of ground-penetrating radar because it demonstrates how autonomous movement can produce meaningful scientific information. Instead of merely travelling across the Moon, the rovers must position themselves carefully as a team so their combined measurements can construct a subsurface map.

Beneficiary groups

Who benefits from CADRE?

01 · Engineering

NASA/JPL engineers

CADRE is intended to provide real-world evidence about the reliability, limitations and failure behaviour of multi-robot autonomy.

Instead of relying entirely on computer simulations, engineers would be able to compare predicted performance with the behaviour of actual rovers in a lunar environment. This could reduce technical uncertainty and inform the design of future planetary robot teams.

02 · Scientific research

Planetary scientists and researchers

CADRE could benefit planetary scientists by demonstrating a new way of collecting scientific data rather than simply carrying another instrument to the Moon. Multiple autonomous rovers can spread across different locations and take simultaneous measurements that a single rover could not obtain alone. In CADRE, coordinated ground-penetrating radar measurements from the three rovers can be combined to construct a 3D representation of the lunar subsurface. If this approach is successful, future scientists could design investigations around distributed measurements, using several mobile instruments to examine different parts of an environment at the same time. This could be particularly valuable for studying large, uneven or difficult-to-access planetary environments.

03 · Future astronaut safety

Astronauts on future missions

Future robotic teams could scout an area before astronauts arrive by:

  • Mapping terrain.
  • Identifying obstacles.
  • Investigating potentially unsafe areas.
  • Determining appropriate travel routes.
  • Locating scientifically or operationally valuable sites.

This could reduce astronauts’ exposure to unstable surfaces, extreme temperatures, radiation and other hazards. NASA identifies astronaut support as a possible application of future multirobot missions.

04 · Potential future application

Environmental researchers

Distributed robots could collect simultaneous environmental measurements across large or inaccessible regions. Possible applications include glaciers, deserts, caves, forests and disaster-affected ecosystems.

Simultaneous measurement is particularly valuable when environmental conditions change quickly, because data gathered at different locations can be compared at the same moment.

These are potential applications of cooperative robotics, rather than activities performed by CADRE’s lunar rovers.

05 · Education and public knowledge

The wider public

CADRE provides an educational example of computing concepts such as:

  • Distributed systems.
  • Autonomous decision-making.
  • Networking and redundancy.
  • Feedback and robotics.
  • Systems engineering.

NASA missions may also generate scientific knowledge and technological innovation funded for public benefit.

06.Software + Hardware Designs

Robotics researchers and developers

CADRE can also benefit researchers developing autonomous and multi-robot systems. The project provides a real mission environment in which technologies such as cooperative planning, navigation, communication, resource management and fault handling can be tested together. JPL also states that it plans to make CADRE's software stack and hardware designs available within the United States under a free licence to help encourage further innovation. This means CADRE's value is not limited to the three flight rovers: knowledge and technology developed by the project could contribute to later autonomous robotic systems.

Development timeline

From remote control to autonomous teams

Select each milestone to explore how planetary rovers developed and where CADRE fits into this evolution.

1958 — Servo Technology Develops

Yaskawa developed the Minertia motor, an important predecessor of modern servo motors. Its high response rate enabled much more precise control of mechanical movement. This represents an early stage in mechatronic development, where electrical control increasingly became integrated with mechanical machinery.

1969 — The Concept of Mechatronics Emerges

Yaskawa engineer Tetsuro Mori coined the term “mechatronics” by combining mechanism and electronics. The trademark application was made in 1969 and registered in 1972. The concept described the integration of mechanical devices such as motors and machines with electronic control to achieve greater functionality.

1970 · Lunokhod 1

The Soviet Lunokhod 1 became the first successful remote-controlled rover on another world. It demonstrated that a wheeled robot could survive and travel across the Moon, although human operators controlled its movements from Earth.

1977 — Programmable Industrial Robotics

Yaskawa introduced the MOTOMAN-L10, Japan's first all-electric industrial robot, in 1977. It integrated electric motors, mechanical joints and programmable control to perform industrial tasks such as arc welding. This demonstrates an important shift: the behaviour of a mechanical machine could increasingly be controlled through electronic programming, rather than being determined solely by its physical design.

1997 · Sojourner

Sojourner became the first rover to operate successfully on Mars. It used six wheels, rocker-bogie suspension and limited autonomous obstacle avoidance. It established the basic mobility design later inherited by NASA’s larger Mars rovers.

2004 · Spirit and Opportunity

These twin Mars rovers were larger than Sojourner, travelled farther and carried more advanced scientific equipment. They used improved autonomous navigation because communication delays prevented continuous control from Earth.

2012–2013 · Curiosity

Curiosity introduced a larger, more powerful rover platform. In 2013, it began using autonomous navigation to analyse stereo images, recognise hazardous terrain and select a safe route beyond areas already examined by operators.

2021 · Perseverance

Perseverance’s improved AutoNav system could process terrain images and make navigation decisions while moving, described as “thinking while driving.” This enabled faster and more independent movement than earlier Mars rovers.

Before CADRE · A-PUFFER

JPL developed A-PUFFER, or Autonomous Pop-Up Flat Folding Explorer Robot. A-PUFFER helped develop an earlier version of the multi-agent autonomous software used by CADRE.

2023 · CADRE team testing

Full-scale CADRE development rovers were tested together in JPL’s Mars Yard. Testing demonstrated that several robots could navigate and operate as a coordinated team.

The photograph shows a separate clean-room driving test in December 2023, when engineers tested the flight rovers’ ability to drive together autonomously.

February 2024 · Flight hardware completed

Construction and environmental testing of CADRE’s flight hardware were completed. CADRE advanced from experimental models to tested flight hardware. Testing included vibration, thermal-vacuum and electromagnetic compatibility testing.

February 2025 · Delivery for integration

The three rovers, deployers and base station were shipped for integration with Intuitive Machines’ lander. This moved CADRE from laboratory development towards a lunar technology demonstration.

Planned lunar demonstration · CADRE

CADRE is designed to demonstrate cooperative robotic autonomy, surface mapping and distributed subsurface radar measurements on the Moon.

Its three rovers will work towards a shared objective, testing whether a robotic team can coordinate its activities with limited direction from Earth.

Future · Larger cooperative robot teams

Larger multirobot planetary systems could be developed using CADRE’s results. Future robots may spread across wider areas, take simultaneous measurements and support astronauts while requiring fewer detailed instructions from Earth.

Future evolution: CADRE's distributed approach demonstrates technologies that could contribute to future teams of robots working together in environments that are distant, hazardous or difficult for humans to access. Rather than relying on a single highly capable machine, future mechatronic systems may increasingly operate as interconnected teams that share information and coordinate tasks autonomously.

Overall evolution

Across this timeline, mechatronic systems evolved from electrically controlled mechanical motion, to programmable machines, to robots capable of sensing and responding to their environments, and eventually to autonomous navigation.

CADRE represents a further development in which autonomy is distributed across multiple cooperating machines. Rather than one robot independently integrating sensing, computation and control, CADRE's rovers exchange information and coordinate their actions towards shared objectives.

The overall progression can therefore be summarised as electrical control → programmable robotics → environmental sensing → autonomous navigation → cooperative distributed autonomy.

How has the technology evolved?

These developments show a progression from remotely controlled machines to rovers that interpret sensor data and make local navigation decisions. CADRE extends this development to cooperative autonomy: several robots must share information, allocate tasks and coordinate their movements to achieve a common objective.

How CADRE works

Inputs, Processes and Outputs

01 · Inputs

High-level commands

Humans provide general objectives, such as exploring a defined region, rather than directly specifying every movement.

Camera images

Cameras provide information used to recognise terrain, locate hazards and construct a three-dimensional surface model.

Ground-penetrating radar returns

Radar transmits signals into the lunar surface and receives reflected signals from subsurface boundaries.

Position and orientation data

Each rover requires an estimate of its location, direction and movement relative to the other rovers and the base station.

Wheel and motor feedback

Information about wheel movement and motor condition helps determine whether commands were completed correctly.

Power data

Battery charge and solar-energy information allow CADRE to determine whether enough power is available for driving, communication, computing and radar operation.

Temperature data

Internal temperature readings allow the system to prevent electronic and mechanical components from operating outside safe thermal limits.

Rover status and task progress

Each rover reports whether it is operating normally and whether its assigned task is pending, underway, completed or unsuccessful.

Communication status

The system monitors whether each rover can communicate with other rovers and the base station. Lunar terrain and electromagnetic interference may disrupt connections.

Shared maps and team data

Each rover receives relevant information gathered by other rovers, including explored locations, detected hazards and important scientific data.

02 · Processes

Terrain perception

CADRE’s onboard software analyses camera images to identify obstacles, slopes and areas that may be safe or unsafe to cross.

Localisation

Each rover processes visual and movement information to estimate its location and orientation. Accurate localisation is necessary because there is no ordinary GPS service on the Moon.

Three-dimensional surface mapping

Images taken from different positions are matched and combined to reconstruct the shape of the lunar terrain. Local maps produced by individual rovers contribute to a shared team map.

Autonomous route planning

The system compares possible paths and determines how each rover can safely reach its assigned destination while avoiding hazardous terrain.

Team planning and task allocation

CADRE converts a broad human objective into smaller tasks and decides which rover should perform each one based on its location, condition, power and other constraints.

Cooperative navigation

The rovers coordinate their routes and movements so they can explore efficiently without colliding or unnecessarily repeating the same work.

Formation control

During the radar experiment, the rovers calculate and adjust their relative positions so that they can take coordinated measurements from different locations.

Radar-data processing

The system records radar reflections together with the position of each rover. Measurements taken from different positions can later be combined to reconstruct subsurface structures.

Power and thermal management

CADRE checks energy and temperature before scheduling activities. It may delay movement or scientific measurements if they would place a rover outside safe operating limits.

Data selection and sharing

Because communication capacity is limited, the system prioritises essential information such as commands, status reports and important mapping data rather than transmitting everything immediately.

Leader selection and fault recovery

One rover performs the main team-planning role. Essential information is preserved so another rover can assume this role if the original leader fails.

Continuous feedback and replanning

CADRE compares the result of each action with the planned result. If a route is blocked, a task fails or a rover’s condition changes, the system can update the team plan.

03 · Outputs

Rover movement

Electrical signals activate the wheel motors, causing each rover to drive, steer, stop or alter its route.

Coordinated formation

The rovers move into specific relative positions required for distributed ground-penetrating radar measurements.

Three-dimensional surface map

Camera information from the rovers is combined to represent the shape of the explored lunar terrain.

Subsurface radar data and map

Coordinated radar measurements provide information that can be used to construct a three-dimensional representation of structures beneath the lunar surface.

Shared team map

Terrain information gathered by individual rovers is combined into a common map used by the autonomous planner.

Task assignments and movement commands

The cooperative planner produces instructions telling each rover what task to complete and where to move. These are internal outputs that become inputs for the individual rover controllers.

Status and fault reports

CADRE produces reports describing rover position, task completion, power, temperature, communication availability and detected problems.

Scientific data sent to Earth

Selected surface images and ground-penetrating radar measurements are transmitted through the base station and lander for analysis by scientists.

Engineering performance data

NASA/JPL engineers receive information showing how successfully the rovers navigated, cooperated, shared data and recovered from problems. This helps evaluate the technology demonstration.

How the feedback loop works

For example, a rover’s cameras detect a rock (input). Navigation software analyses the terrain and calculates a safer route (process). Motor commands steer the rover around the obstacle (output). New images and position estimates show whether the movement succeeded (feedback). The system can then correct its route if necessary.

System requirements

What must CADRE do, and how well?

Functional requirements

Functional requirements describe what CADRE must do to achieve its mission.

FR01

Autonomous task planning and allocation

CADRE must convert an overall instruction from Earth, such as exploring a designated region, into tasks for individual rovers. An elected leader coordinates the plan while each rover carries out its assigned activities. This allows the team to pursue a shared objective without operators specifying every movement.

FR02

Navigation and hazard avoidance

Each rover must use sensor information to assess its surroundings, identify obstacles and determine a safe route. When new hazards appear, the system must update its plans. This feedback is essential because the actual terrain may differ from the information available when a route was first selected.

FR03

Surface mapping and information sharing

The rovers must collect camera observations to construct maps and share relevant information. This enables observations made by one rover to inform the team’s decisions, improving coordination and helping the robots avoid previously identified hazards.

FR04

Formation driving and coordinated measurements

CADRE must coordinate the rovers’ movements during experiments that require them to travel together. Their ground-penetrating radars must also take coordinated measurements from different positions. These functions allow the team to gather data that depends on several instruments operating together.

FR05

Communication and data return

The rovers must exchange information about their activities and condition through their radio network. They must also communicate with the lander’s base station, which provides the connection to Earth. Returning measurements and operational information allows engineers to evaluate whether the demonstration achieved its objectives.

FR06

Resource monitoring and fault response

CADRE must monitor battery energy, temperature and task progress, then adjust activities when necessary. It must also support the selection of a replacement leader if the existing leader becomes unavailable. These functions help the team respond to changing conditions instead of continuing with an unsuitable plan.

Non-functional requirements

Non-functional requirements describe the quality of CADRE’s operation and the constraints it must satisfy.

NFR01

Reliability and fault tolerance

The system must perform consistently during its intended mission and manage some failures without unnecessarily losing the entire team’s capabilities. Leader replacement supports this quality. However, multiple rovers do not eliminate every risk, particularly failures affecting shared infrastructure such as the lander’s communication link.

NFR02

Environmental durability

The hardware must withstand launch vibration, vacuum and the temperatures encountered during the mission. NASA conducted vibration and thermal-vacuum testing to assess these capabilities. Durability matters because physical damage could prevent the system from performing its functions regardless of the quality of its software.

NFR03

Energy efficiency and thermal performance

CADRE must balance driving, sensing and computing against limited power and cooling capacity. Its planned wake–sleep cycles provide opportunities to recharge and release heat. This creates a design trade-off: increasing activity or computation may improve immediate productivity but also increase the risk of overheating or exhausting available energy.

NFR04

Positioning and measurement accuracy

Position estimates, camera observations and radar timing must be sufficiently accurate for coordinated exploration. Errors could affect formation driving or reduce the usefulness of combined measurements. Accuracy therefore influences both safe movement and the quality of the information produced.

NFR05

Compactness and low mass

Each rover must accommodate its instruments, computer, power supply and mobility equipment within a small, lightweight structure suitable for delivery to the Moon. This constrains component selection: additional equipment may increase capability while also increasing mass, power demand and integration difficulty.

NFR06

Electromagnetic compatibility

Electronic subsystems must work together without unacceptable interference, including interference with the lander. NASA tested this specifically. A component that operates correctly in isolation may still compromise the mission if its emissions disrupt another instrument or communication system.

NFR07

Suitability for the intended operating lifetime

CADRE is designed for approximately 14 Earth days of lunar daylight, with survival through lunar night not expected. Its reliability and durability should therefore be evaluated against that planned duration. Demonstrating successful short-term operation would not establish suitability for a mission lasting months or years.

Impacts

Advantages, disadvantages and employment

CADRE tests whether robotic teams can carry out coordinated work with less human supervision. Its advantages include new scientific capabilities and potential safety benefits, while its limitations involve technical complexity, dependence on shared infrastructure and uncertain long-term costs. Because CADRE is a technology demonstration, many broader impacts remain potential outcomes for future missions.

Advantages

What benefits could CADRE provide?

  • Expanded scientific capability: By taking measurements from different locations simultaneously, CADRE can collect information that a single rover could not obtain alone. Its coordinated ground-penetrating radars are intended to investigate the Moon’s subsurface in three dimensions. This demonstrates how cooperation can increase the range of scientific investigations available to researchers, rather than simply increase the quantity of data collected.
  • Reduced need for detailed human control: Operators provide an overall objective, while the system coordinates individual tasks and responds to changing conditions. This could reduce the workload involved in directing several robots separately, allowing mission teams to focus more on scientific priorities and interpreting results. However, this is a potential improvement in operational efficiency, not evidence of a confirmed reduction in staffing or total mission cost.
  • Potentially safer exploration: NASA identifies hazardous-terrain exploration and astronaut support as possible applications of future cooperative rover teams. Robots could assess unfamiliar areas before people enter them, helping mission planners identify hazards while limiting human exposure. Equipment can undertake some exploratory risks that would be less acceptable for astronauts.
  • Resilience when individual robots encounter problems: CADRE can replace an unavailable leader and revise the team’s plan. This reduces dependence on one permanently designated coordinating robot and could preserve useful activity when part of the system experiences difficulties. Nevertheless, losing a rover may still reduce coverage or limit experiments requiring multiple instruments.
  • Greater access to research and participation: Sharing mission findings, methods and usable data could allow universities and students without their own lunar missions to benefit from the research. NASA’s broader Artemis principles support public release of scientific results. The extent of this benefit would depend on accessible documentation and the resources needed to analyse the information; publishing data alone does not ensure that everyone can use it effectively.
  • Greater coverage and parallel operation: A team of autonomous rovers can spread across an area and perform activities in parallel rather than requiring one rover to visit every location sequentially. NASA states that multiple small autonomous robots could cover more ground and potentially help map previously unexplored regions. This distributed approach could therefore increase the area investigated within a limited mission period while also allowing measurements to be collected from several locations simultaneously.
Disadvantages

What limitations and risks remain?

  • Greater development and testing demands: Coordinating several robots introduces more interactions that engineers must verify. A rover may operate correctly by itself yet behave unexpectedly when responding to other robots. NASA’s extensive hardware and software testing illustrates the effort needed to address these challenges. Automation can reduce some routine operational work while increasing the specialist effort required before deployment.
  • Communication problems can reduce team effectiveness: Cooperative exploration depends on exchanging relevant information. JPL identifies obstructions and lunar surface conditions as challenges for communication between the robots. If information is delayed or unavailable, the system may need to revise or postpone coordinated activities. Consequently, adding more robots does not automatically produce a proportional increase in productivity.
  • Shared infrastructure remains a vulnerability: The rovers depend on the lander for communication with Earth. Therefore, even functioning rovers could lose the ability to return results if that shared connection fails. This shows the limit of redundancy: having several mobile robots protects against some individual failures but cannot remove every mission-wide point of failure.
  • Autonomous errors can have physical consequences: As an engineering risk, an incorrect interpretation of terrain could produce an unsuitable route or movement. Without a human approving every action, the system needs reliable constraints and recovery behaviour. NASA tested responses to unexpected obstacles and slippery surfaces, demonstrating why safe adaptation matters alongside successful task completion.
  • Limited lifetime restricts the evidence gained: CADRE’s planned operation covers approximately 14 Earth days of lunar daylight, and survival through lunar night is not expected. A successful demonstration would therefore provide evidence for those operating conditions, rather than establish that the same design could support long-duration exploration. Its short lifetime also raises an environmental consideration: developers should evaluate the value gained against the resources used and equipment remaining on the lunar surface.
  • Trade offs caused by compact design: CADRE's small size makes the rovers easier to transport and allows several robots to be deployed together, but it also limits the resources available for computing, power and thermal management. NASA describes a difficult balance between keeping each rover compact and lightweight while providing enough computing power to run cooperative autonomy. The processor itself generates heat, adding to the challenge of operating in the hot lunar environment. CADRE therefore uses repeated wake-sleep cycles to manage temperature and recharge its batteries. This demonstrates that miniaturisation can increase deployment flexibility while also imposing significant engineering constraints.

Employment and job losses

CADRE may change the type of human work required, but the reviewed sources do not establish that it has caused job losses. Its development involved NASA centres, commercial hardware specialists and university researchers.

A reasonable inference is that increasing autonomy could reduce repetitive control tasks while increasing the importance of programming, testing, fault diagnosis and scientific interpretation. These roles require different skills, so any wider adoption should be accompanied by training.

Whether employment ultimately increases or decreases would depend on how organisations use the technology and whether expanded exploration creates additional work.

Responsible development

Social, Legal and Ethical Considerations

S

Social considerations

Fair access to scientific knowledge

CADRE’s developers should consider how its findings can benefit people beyond the organisations involved in the mission. NASA’s Artemis principles support the public release of scientific results.

Publishing well-documented data, explanations and educational resources would help universities and students without access to lunar missions participate in the research. However, access must be practical: highly technical information without suitable documentation may remain usable only by well-resourced specialists.

Changes to employment and skills

Autonomous coordination could change operators’ responsibilities, placing greater emphasis on supervising systems, diagnosing faults and interpreting results. Developers should involve operators in interface design and provide training so they understand when to trust automated decisions and when intervention is needed.

There is no evidence in the sources reviewed that CADRE itself has caused job losses; the relevant consideration is preparing people for changing work. Worker participation and training should be considered when introducing automation.

Public trust and responsible funding

As a publicly funded technology demonstration, CADRE should have clearly explained objectives and transparent reporting of results. Developers should distinguish demonstrated capabilities from future possibilities, particularly when discussing astronaut support or reduced operating costs.

This allows the public to assess the value of the research. Reporting setbacks also matters: explaining what engineers learned from a failure can demonstrate accountability without presenting every experiment as a complete success.

Interoperability with other lunar systems

As lunar exploration involves increasing numbers of government and commerical organisations, future autonomous robots may need to operate alongside equipment developed by different organisations. Developers should therefore consider compatible communication, navigation and operational standards rather than designed systems entirely in insolation.

The Artemis Accords encourage participating nations to use existing interoperability standards and support the development of new standards where necessary. For technologies developed from CADRE, interoperability could become increasingly important as multiple robotic and human systems operate within the same lunar environment.

L

Legal considerations

Responsibility and liability

Article VI of the Outer Space Treaty establishes state responsibility for national space activities, including private activities. Article VII addresses launching-state liability for specified damage caused by space objects.

CADRE’s autonomy does not remove these responsibilities. Mission organisations should define decision authority and preserve operational records to support investigation if damage occurs.

Avoiding harmful interference and contamination

Article IX of the Outer Space Treaty requires due regard for other states’ interests and avoidance of harmful contamination, with consultation where potentially harmful interference is anticipated.

Developers should assess routes, disturbed dust and instrument operations for possible effects on other missions or investigations.

Peaceful use and no territorial claims

Article IV of the Outer Space Treaty requires peaceful use of the Moon, while Article II prohibits national appropriation. CADRE’s mapping activities therefore do not establish national ownership of the explored area.

Mission boundaries should serve operational safety rather than territorial exclusion.

E

Ethical considerations

Accountability for autonomous decisions

Developers should ensure that an unexpected action can be investigated through records of sensor readings, instructions and planning decisions. Responsibility should remain clearly assigned to the organisations and people designing and operating the system.

For example, if a rover repeatedly selects unsuitable routes, engineers should be able to determine whether the cause is inaccurate sensing, planning logic or inappropriate operating limits. This is a recommended application of responsible risk management, rather than a claim about CADRE’s exact logging arrangements.

Safety before task completion

The system should operate within defined limits and pause or revise activities when conditions become unsafe. NASA tested CADRE’s responses to unexpected obstacles and slippery surfaces, demonstrating the importance of handling situations that differ from the original plan.

Ethically, developers should evaluate foreseeable failures as carefully as successful operation: completing an exploration task should not justify avoidable damage to equipment or risks to others.

Cybersecurity and data integrity

Developers should protect the system against unauthorised commands and altered information. In a mechatronic system, compromised data can influence physical movement, so cybersecurity is also a safety consideration.

Recommended measures include controlled command access, checks on received messages and testing responses to invalid information. These are proposed safeguards; the public sources reviewed do not establish CADRE’s precise security implementation.

Protecting the lunar environment and future research

CADRE is designed for a short daylight mission, with lunar-night survival not expected. Developers should therefore consider the equipment remaining on the surface and minimise unnecessary disturbance.

The ethical issue extends beyond immediate mission success: later researchers should still be able to investigate scientifically valuable areas. This suggests documenting disturbed locations and weighing the expected knowledge gained against the mission’s physical footprint.

Present and future significance

How could automated systems shape our future?

Why mechatronic systems matter today

Mechatronic systems combine mechanical components, electronics, sensors and computer control. By using information from their surroundings to control physical actions, they can perform tasks with speed, precision and consistency.

In manufacturing, automated machines perform repetitive tasks such as assembling and inspecting products. Modern automation can also improve the operation of production systems: McKinsey reports that successful Industry 4.0 implementations have been associated with 30–50% reductions in machine downtime, 10–30% increases in throughput and 15–30% improvements in labour productivity. This demonstrates why mechatronic systems are increasingly important not simply for replacing manual movement, but for making industrial processes more responsive and efficient.

Mechatronics is also significant beyond manufacturing. In agriculture, sensors can monitor environmental conditions and provide information for automated irrigation or machinery. In hazardous environments, remotely operated or autonomous robots can inspect locations that would expose people to danger. In transport, mechatronic systems integrate sensors, electronic controllers and mechanical components in technologies such as anti-lock braking and autonomous emergency braking.

These systems can improve productivity and reduce unnecessary resource use. However, their environmental benefits depend on how they are designed and used. Manufacturing electronics, supplying electricity and disposing of damaged equipment also consume resources and create waste.

Where CADRE fits

CADRE explores a further development: several robots coordinating their actions towards a shared objective. Each rover has limited power, sensing and mobility, but exchanging information allows the team to plan together and collect measurements from different locations.

Its significance is therefore both technical and practical. It tests whether cooperative autonomy could make exploration less dependent on detailed human instructions while revealing the challenges of communication, coordination and fault recovery.

Possible future applications

01

Planetary exploration: Future robot teams could divide an exploration area, share observations and collect simultaneous measurements. This could support investigations that would be difficult for one rover alone.

02

Astronaut support: Cooperative robots could survey routes and investigate hazards before astronauts enter an area. This could help crews make better-informed decisions about where to travel and work.

03

Search and rescue: Similar approaches could allow robots to explore different parts of a damaged building and share a map with rescuers. Unstable structures and unreliable communication would remain challenges.

04

Environmental monitoring: Coordinated robots could collect measurements across forests, waterways or disaster-affected areas. Comparing observations from several locations could help researchers track changes.

05

Changing human work: People may increasingly set objectives and supervise automated systems while machines handle detailed actions. Training would help workers move into programming, maintenance, analysis and oversight.

06

From individual robots to cooperative intelligence: CADRE demonstrates an important direction in the evolution of mechatronic and automated systems: intelligence does not have to be concentrated within one increasingly complex machine. Instead, several smaller machines can combine mechanical mobility, sensing, onboard computation and communication to create distributed intelligence. Each CADRE rover remains an individual mechatronic system, but communication and cooperative autonomy allow the three robots to function as a larger coordinated system.

This approach could influence future applications beyond lunar exploration. Teams of autonomous robots could distribute themselves across hazardous or inaccessible environments, divide tasks according to their locations and conditions, share discoveries and adapt their plans when conditions change. Potential applications include planetary exploration, disaster assessment and environmental monitoring. CADRE’s broader significance therefore lies in demonstrating a possible transition from single intelligent machines toward networks of cooperating intelligent machines.

Mechatronic and automated systems could improve safety, productivity and environmental monitoring. However, these benefits are not automatic. Their long-term value depends on reliable operation, responsible resource use, accessible training and clear human accountability.

  • Explore adding clickable explanations of rover components.
  • Expandable panels for detailed information.
  • A feedback example linking sensor information, decisions and movement.
  • Clear hover and keyboard-focus states for interactive elements.
Branch 05

Accessibility and responsive design

  • Comfortable text size and readable line lengths.
  • Clear contrast between text and backgrounds.
  • Descriptive alternative text and image captions.
  • Keyboard access to links and controls.
  • Cards that stack vertically on small screens.
  • Images and a model viewer that fit the screen.
  • Buttons large enough for touch controls.
  • Avoid distracting motion and automatic audio.
  • Plan a rover image if the 3D model cannot load.
Branch 06

Development and evaluation

  • Sketch the layout and organise research.
  • Use HTML to structure the content.
  • Use reusable CSS for appearance and layout.
  • Add JavaScript where interaction requires it.
  • Use VS Code for coding and Blender for modelling.
  • Save versions and track changes through GitHub.
  • Check sources and distinguish planned capabilities from demonstrated results.
  • Test navigation, images, interactions and different screen sizes.
  • Ask a classmate to find information and identify confusing areas.
  • Proofread, check the assessment criteria and complete the bibliography.
